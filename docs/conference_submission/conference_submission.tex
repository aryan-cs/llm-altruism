\title{Prosocial Readiness Bench: Safety Refusal, Cooperation, and Commons Restraint in LLM Agents}

\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
As LLMs move beyond chatbot interfaces into agents that use tools, coordinate with other systems, and interact with real-world workflows, evaluations need to cover more than harmful-request refusal. Current evaluations often measure refusal separately from social-choice behavior, leaving unclear whether models that avoid explicit harm also cooperate under temptation or conserve shared resources over time. We introduce Prosocial Readiness Bench, an executable benchmark for observable prosocial behavior with three task contracts: safety refusal on multilingual harmful-request prompts, dyadic cooperation across one-shot social dilemmas, and commons restraint in repeated 50-agent resource simulations. The current release evaluates 13 local/open-weight model variants over 3,861 harmful-request judgments, 4,992 dyadic decisions, and 47,529 commons agent-day decisions, with prompts, schemas, metadata sidecars, manifests, validation reports, Croissant metadata, derived tables, figures, and reproduction commands. The pilot demonstrates the value of keeping the axes separate: refusal spans 9.4--99.3\%, cooperation spans 24.2--75.0\%, restraint spans 18.6--92.5\%, frame changes cooperation from 83.2\% under observer evaluation to 4.9\% under prediction, and commons restraint is strongly associated with final simulated population. Prosocial Readiness Bench provides a traceable artifact for pre-deployment behavioral auditing of actions and justifications, while keeping claims grounded in observable benchmark behavior.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
LLM agents increasingly operate in social settings: they answer human requests, recommend actions, coordinate with other systems, and may participate in workflows where individual and collective incentives diverge. In these settings, refusal of explicitly harmful requests is only one part of responsible behavior. A broader evaluation should also assess whether a model selects cooperative actions in social dilemmas and whether repeated choices preserve a shared resource when overuse offers a short-term advantage.

Prosocial Readiness Bench addresses this requirement through a reproducible benchmark. The artifact measures three observable behaviors rather than a latent trait: Part 0 measures safety refusal, Part 1 measures dyadic cooperation, and Part 2 measures commons restraint in a repeated simulation. The benchmark is designed for behavioral auditing: it records the action selected by the model, the stated justification when available, the metadata needed to trace a run, and the derived metrics used in the paper.

The paper asks two concrete questions. First, can refusal, cooperation, and commons restraint be measured in one release-grade artifact with explicit schemas, validation, and reproduction commands? Second, do these behaviors move together across a local/open-weight model cohort, or do they expose separable behavioral patterns that a single score would hide? Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the artifact flow.

The pilot evaluation covers 13 local/open-weight model variants. It shows wide and informative variation on all three axes: safety refusal ranges from 9.4\% to 99.3\%, dyadic cooperation from 24.2\% to 75.0\%, and commons restraint from 18.6\% to 92.5\%. Seven of thirteen same-model societies preserve a nonzero population through the 100-day horizon. Cross-part diagnostics show a weak association between refusal and cooperation (Pearson $r=0.24$), a stronger association between refusal and restraint ($r=0.77$), and a strong association between restraint and final population ($r=0.85$). Part 1 also exposes a large elicitation effect: cooperation is 83.2\% under observer-evaluation prompts and 4.9\% under prediction prompts.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benchmark artifact.} We release Prosocial Readiness Bench, a three-part evaluation for safety refusal, dyadic cooperation, and commons restraint under explicit action contracts.
    \item \textbf{Traceable release package.} We provide prompts, schemas, metadata sidecars, manifests, validation output, derived tables, figures, and reproduction commands aligned with the reported claims.
    \item \textbf{Open-model pilot.} We report 56,382 decisions or judgments across 13 local/open-weight model variants, using Wilson intervals for rates and bootstrap intervals for model-level cross-part correlations.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic findings.} We show that refusal, cooperation, frame sensitivity, commons restraint, and survival capture different aspects of observable agent behavior.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work and Gap Matrix}
Harmful-request and jailbreak benchmarks such as HarmBench and JailbreakBench provide standardized ways to evaluate whether models comply with dangerous instructions or resist adversarial wrappers \citep{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}. Prosocial Readiness Bench builds on this safety-refusal tradition by placing refusal next to two additional social-choice measurements.

Social-dilemma theory studies cooperation when private incentives conflict with group welfare \citep{axelrod1984evolution,hardin1968tragedy,ostrom1990governing}. Recent LLM-agent work shows that language models can participate in role-based and multi-agent settings \citep{park2023generative}. The benchmark uses this tradition to define controlled dyadic and repeated-resource choices whose action semantics are explicit and programmatically scored.

Broader trust, preference, and alignment datasets illustrate the value of multi-axis evaluation and careful artifact documentation \citep{wang2023decodingtrust,kirk2024prism,jiang2025artificialhivemind}. Prosocial Readiness Bench follows that pattern for agentic social behavior: it treats task definitions, metadata, validation, and diagnostic breakdowns as part of the artifact, not as post hoc analysis.

\section{Benchmark Design and Task Contracts}
\subsection{Design Principles}
The benchmark uses three design principles.

\paragraph{Measure behaviors separately.}
Safety refusal, dyadic cooperation, and commons restraint are related deployment-relevant behaviors, but they are not interchangeable. The benchmark therefore reports separate primary metrics instead of collapsing them into one prosociality or readiness score.

\paragraph{Make each claim traceable.}
Each run produces structured CSV output and metadata sidecars. The analysis pipeline writes derived tables, validation reports, and figures from those files, so paper claims can be audited against concrete artifacts.

\paragraph{Keep trace-level outputs.}
The benchmark stores actions, justifications, run metadata, and derived summaries so downstream users can audit each reported claim without relying on plot files alone.

\subsection{Part 0: Safety Refusal}
Part 0 presents harmful-request prompts and records whether the model refuses or materially complies. The current raw CSV schema stores \texttt{provider}, \texttt{model}, \texttt{language}, \texttt{prompt}, \texttt{reasoning}, \texttt{response}, and \texttt{complied?}. The release contains 1,287 English, 1,287 Chinese, and 1,287 Russian rows. Prompt provenance is documented through the experiment code and release documentation.

The primary metric is safety refusal:
\[
\mathrm{refusal} =
\frac{\#\mathrm{denied}}{\#\mathrm{denied}+\#\mathrm{complied}}.
\]
This metric gives a direct, auditable measure of refusal on the harmful-request set and provides the safety axis used in cross-part analysis.

\subsection{Part 1: Dyadic Cooperation}
Part 1 evaluates one-shot social-dilemma choices. The prompt matrix has two game families, four frames, six domains, four scenario variants per domain-game pair, and two presentation styles, yielding 384 decisions per model. The game families use \texttt{COOPERATE}/\texttt{DEFECT} and \texttt{RESTRAIN}/\texttt{OVERUSE} action pairs. The frames ask for direct self choice, advice, observer evaluation, or prediction.

The primary metric is dyadic cooperation:
\[
\mathrm{cooperation} =
\frac{\#\mathrm{cooperative}}{\#\mathrm{cooperative}+\#\mathrm{noncooperative}}.
\]
\texttt{COOPERATE} and \texttt{RESTRAIN} are scored as cooperative actions; \texttt{DEFECT} and \texttt{OVERUSE} are scored as non-cooperative actions. The frame, domain, game, variant, and presentation fields make prompt sensitivity measurable rather than implicit.

\subsection{Part 2: Commons Restraint}
Part 2 creates same-model societies in a repeated common-pool resource simulation. Each configured run starts with 50 active agents, a 100-day horizon, and a water reserve with capacity 2,500 units. \texttt{OPTION\_A} maps to \texttt{RESTRAIN}: it uses a 1x daily score multiplier and leaves the reserve unchanged. \texttt{OPTION\_B} maps to \texttt{OVERUSE}: it gives a higher daily score multiplier and reduces the reserve by 2 units. The prompt includes the current day, living population, reserve state, and previous-day \texttt{OPTION\_B} count.

The primary action metric is commons restraint:
\[
\mathrm{restraint} =
\frac{\#\mathrm{restrain}}{\#\mathrm{restrain}+\#\mathrm{overuse}}.
\]
Part 2 also reports final reserve, first depletion day, final population, total deaths, and survival through the configured horizon. This makes repeated local choices visible as aggregate population and resource trajectories.

\section{Artifact Construction and Quality Control}
\paragraph{Model cohort.}
The pilot evaluates 13 local/open-weight variants through Ollama. The cohort includes standard, safeguard, instruct, uncensored, and derestricted variants across GPT-OSS, Llama, Qwen 2.5, and Qwen 3.5 families. This cohort is suitable for benchmark validation because it spans safety-oriented and less-restricted release styles, producing variation across all three parts.

\paragraph{Hardware and model-selection constraints.}
The reported runs used local Ollama execution in a containerized environment with one NVIDIA H200 NVL GPU. The effective runtime constraint was the cgroup allocation: 32 GiB system RAM, no swap, and a 10-CPU quota, despite a larger host machine. We therefore selected local/open-weight variants that could be run repeatedly under those limits. Part 2 is especially inference-heavy: a full 50-agent, 100-day commons run can require up to 5,000 model decisions for one model. These constraints explain why the current cohort emphasizes locally runnable 7B--20B variants and release-style comparisons rather than frontier-scale or closed-source model coverage.

\paragraph{Output contracts and metadata.}
Part 0 stores model outputs and a normalized compliance verdict. Part 1 requires a valid action and justification. Part 2 requires a daily action and reasoning field for each living agent. Completed runs are paired with metadata sidecars recording available provider/model identifiers, command context, timestamps, prompt or configuration hashes when available, experiment settings, run status, and output paths. Legacy metadata is backfilled where possible and marked as reconstructed when necessary. The release also includes a Croissant JSON-LD metadata file documenting derived tables, validation reports, manifests, access conditions, safety handling, and responsible-use constraints.

\paragraph{Validation.}
Validation is graph-independent. It checks CSV headers, duplicate rows, missing required fields, invalid actions, prompt-matrix coverage, Part 2 day continuity, incomplete days, reserve and population transition consistency, and interrupted versus complete status. The current validation report covers 27 CSV files: one Part 0 file, 13 Part 1 files, and 13 Part 2 files, with 0 failures.

\paragraph{Harmful-output handling.}
Part 0 evaluates harmful prompts and may produce unsafe completions. The anonymous supplement therefore emphasizes derived tables, validation outputs, and aggregate summaries rather than raw harmful generations. This release choice preserves the evidence used in the paper while limiting unnecessary redistribution of harmful text.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
For each model and part, the analysis pipeline computes the primary rate, Wilson 95\% confidence interval, and task-specific diagnostics. Part 2 additionally computes first depletion day, final reserve, final population, and total deaths. Cross-part analysis joins the three model-level tables by model identifier and reports Pearson and Spearman correlations with bootstrap intervals.

The reported comparisons are descriptive pilot results over 13 related local/open-weight variants. The paper uses them to demonstrate benchmark behavior, reveal diagnostic variation, and generate hypotheses for broader confirmatory evaluation. Family-level readings below group models by visible release lineage and should be interpreted as descriptive clusters, not independent estimates of vendor or architecture effects.

\paragraph{Refusal separates safety-oriented and less-restricted variants.}
Part 0 refusal ranges from 9.4\% to 99.3\%. Several standard or safeguard-oriented variants refuse nearly all harmful prompts, while explicitly uncensored or derestricted variants show much lower refusal. The within-family splits are visible: GPT-OSS and GPT-OSS-Safeguard refuse at 99.3\% and 97.0\%, while the derestricted GPT-OSS variant drops to 16.2\%. The safeguard variant therefore does not outperform the standard GPT-OSS model on this safety-refusal benchmark; it is slightly lower by 2.3 percentage points, although that gap is small compared with the 80.8-point gap between standard and derestricted GPT-OSS. Qwen 3.5 shows a similar safety-release split, with base and instruct variants at 98.3\% and 97.6\% but uncensored variants at 32.7\% and 9.4\%. Qwen 2.5 is lower in its base and instruct forms (74.4\% and 64.3\%), and the Qwen 2.5 abliterate variants fall to 27.9--29.0\%. Llama 2 also moves downward under the uncensored tag, from 77.4\% to 51.2\%. These results establish a safety-refusal axis for cross-part comparison.

\paragraph{Cooperation is strongly frame-sensitive.}
Part 1 cooperation ranges from 24.2\% to 75.0\%. The principal Part 1 pattern is the frame effect: across models, cooperation is 83.2\% under observer-evaluation prompts, 66.8\% under advice prompts, 61.4\% under self-direct prompts, and 4.9\% under prediction prompts. The model-family pattern differs from refusal. Qwen 2.5 base, instruct, and abliterate variants all sit near the top at 74.0--75.0\%, while Qwen 3.5 separates into a cooperative base model (71.1\%), a mid-range instruct variant (48.7\%), and low-cooperation uncensored variants (25.8\% and 24.2\%). Llama 2 reverses the safety pattern, with the uncensored variant cooperating more than the base model (68.8\% versus 52.6\%). GPT-OSS variants remain lower overall, but here the safeguard variant is the strongest GPT-OSS run: 47.1\% cooperation versus 38.5\% for standard GPT-OSS and 27.1\% for derestricted GPT-OSS. The benchmark therefore distinguishes socially preferred judgments from expectations about how an agent will behave.

\paragraph{Commons restraint produces survival differences.}
Part 2 restraint ranges from 18.6\% to 92.5\%. Seven of thirteen same-model societies preserve a nonzero population through the 100-day horizon. The highest-restraint runs also preserve substantial reserve capacity, while lower-restraint runs deplete the reserve earlier and produce full population collapse. Thus, the GPT-OSS safeguard label is not uniformly better across all axes, but it does improve commons restraint relative to standard GPT-OSS (71.2\% versus 63.1\%) and substantially relative to derestricted GPT-OSS (34.5\%). \section{Diagnostics}
\paragraph{The constructs dissociate.}
The cross-part results support separate reporting. Safety refusal and dyadic cooperation have a weak association in this cohort (Pearson $r=0.24$, bootstrap interval $[-0.61,0.70]$). Safety refusal and commons restraint are more strongly associated (Pearson $r=0.77$, interval $[0.51,0.90]$). Cooperation and restraint are positive but uncertain (Pearson $r=0.34$, interval $[-0.25,0.94]$). These results show why the artifact reports multiple axes rather than a single aggregate readiness score.

\paragraph{Family profiles are not one-dimensional.}
The named release families reinforce the same point. Qwen 2.5 base and instruct variants are high on cooperation and commons restraint, but Qwen 2.5 abliterate variants keep high Part 1 cooperation while collapsing in Part 2. Qwen 3.5 base and instruct variants show high safety refusal and survive the commons, while Qwen 3.5 uncensored variants are low across all three axes. Llama 2 uncensored lowers refusal relative to Llama 2 but raises dyadic cooperation, and both Llama societies survive. GPT-OSS base and safeguard variants remain high-refusal but do not preserve a full commons population; within that pair, the safeguard variant is slightly lower on safety refusal but higher on cooperation and commons restraint. These profiles support model-family analysis, while also cautioning against using family labels as a substitute for axis-level measurement.

\paragraph{Framing changes measured cooperation.}
Part 1 treats prompt frame as an experimental variable. Observer-evaluation prompts elicit high cooperative judgments, advice and self-direct prompts are intermediate, and prediction prompts elicit low cooperative predictions. This split is useful for auditing because a model can know which action is socially preferred while predicting that agents will act selfishly.

\caption{Part 1 cooperation by model and frame. Each cell is a cooperation rate, showing that elicitation frame is a first-class benchmark dimension.}

Figure~\ref{fig:frame-effects} shows that the prediction column reflects a distinct elicitation condition rather than a scoring artifact. Nearly every model predicts selfish immediate-payoff behavior under the prediction frame, even when it cooperates under self-direct, advice, or observer-evaluation frames. \texttt{llama2-uncensored} is the main exception, with 58.3\% prediction-frame cooperation, while \path{qwen2.5:7b}, \path{qwen2.5:7b-instruct}, \path{qwen3.5}, and both Qwen 2.5 abliterate variants are nearly saturated on the non-prediction frames. The uncensored Qwen 3.5 variants have lower cooperation across frames, especially under self-direct prompts.

\paragraph{Restraint predicts survival in the simulated commons.}
Part 2 ties local overuse choices to aggregate outcomes. Commons restraint is strongly associated with final population (Pearson $r=0.85$, bootstrap interval $[0.68,0.98]$). The relationship is expected from the transition rules and supports auditing by representing cumulative overuse as resource depletion and population loss.

Figure~\ref{fig:cross-diagnostics} shows both association and exceptions. High refusal does not guarantee high dyadic cooperation or full commons preservation: standard GPT-OSS and GPT-OSS-Safeguard are high-refusal but only midrange on cooperation and do not preserve a full 50-agent commons population. Low-refusal uncensored and derestricted variants mostly occupy the lower-restraint region, while the Qwen 2.5 abliterate models provide the strongest counterexample to a simple monotonic interpretation because they pair low refusal with high Part 1 cooperation and poor Part 2 restraint.

Figure~\ref{fig:restraint-population} shows a stronger action-outcome relationship because it reflects the Part 2 transition rules. Societies above roughly three-quarters restraint generally survive, while low-restraint Qwen 2.5 abliterate, Qwen 3.5 uncensored, and GPT-OSS derestricted runs collapse. Differences among high-restraint survivors remain substantively important: \path{sorc/qwen3.5-instruct} survives with only 10 reserve units, whereas the Qwen 2.5 and Llama high-restraint runs preserve substantially more capacity.

\section{Scope and Responsible Use}
Prosocial Readiness Bench is an evaluation artifact for observable actions and justifications. Its current release is strongest as a reproducible open-model pilot and benchmark package. The model cohort is local/open-weight, several variants are related, and local/Ollama tags can vary across installations unless exact revisions or digests are frozen. These factors affect leaderboard-style interpretation, but they do not affect the artifact contribution: the task contracts, schemas, validation checks, and analysis pipeline remain reusable for broader model cohorts, including larger open-weight models, stronger hosted systems, and closed-source API models.

The three benchmark parts also define clear scope boundaries. Part 0 uses automated compliance judgments that should be further calibrated for borderline, multilingual, or refusal-style cases. Part 1 evaluates artificial one-shot scenarios, making it well suited for controlled frame and domain comparisons but not a substitute for observing real institutions. Part 2 uses homogeneous same-model societies and one resource dynamic, making action-to-outcome trajectories easy to audit while leaving heterogeneous agents, institutions, shocks, and richer incentives for future extensions. Reasoning text is treated as process evidence rather than a complete semantic account of model behavior.

The documented roadmap targets these omitted pressures directly. Future extensions would add public reputation and rating signals to the commons, testing whether social visibility, public accountability, and strategic image management change restraint. They would also expand from a binary commons choice to open-world societies with persistent health, hunger, thirst, energy, happiness, inventories, foraging, trade, theft, violence, and reproduction. Combining open-world dynamics with reputation would then test trust formation, sanctioning, partner selection, exclusion, and whether cooperation persists under richer reputational incentives.

The safety component requires careful release handling because harmful prompts can elicit unsafe completions. The anonymous supplement therefore provides the evidence needed for the paper's aggregate claims while limiting redistribution of raw harmful generations. The benchmark should be used to identify behavioral risks and compare mitigation strategies, not to improve harmful compliance.

\section{Conclusion}
Prosocial Readiness Bench provides a concrete, reproducible way to evaluate three observable behaviors that matter for LLM agents in social settings: refusing harmful requests, cooperating in dyadic dilemmas, and preserving a shared resource under repeated incentive conflict. The pilot shows that these axes reveal different model profiles, that prompt frame substantially changes measured cooperation, and that commons restraint is closely tied to simulated survival. The release packages the prompts, schemas, metadata, validation checks, tables, figures, and reproduction commands needed to audit and extend the benchmark. A natural next step is a frozen confirmatory evaluation with broader model coverage across more open-weight families, stronger models, and closed-source/API systems, alongside repeated decoding settings, stronger judge calibration, reputation-aware commons environments, richer open-world societies, and preregistered hypotheses about trust, sanctioning, partner selection, and survival under social accountability.
