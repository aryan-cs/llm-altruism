\title{Prosocial Readiness Bench: Safety Refusal, Cooperation, and Commons Restraint in LLM Agents}

\author{Anonymous Authors}

\begin{document}

\begin{abstract}
Evaluations of LLM agents typically measure harmful-request refusal in isolation, leaving open whether models that avoid explicit harm also cooperate under temptation or conserve shared resources over time.
We introduce \textbf{Prosocial Readiness Bench}, a three-part benchmark that jointly measures safety refusal on multilingual harmful-request prompts, dyadic cooperation across one-shot social dilemmas, and commons restraint in repeated 50-agent resource simulations.
A pilot over 13 local/open-weight model variants (56,382 total decisions) shows that these axes dissociate: refusal spans 9.4--99.3\%, cooperation 24.2--75.0\%, and restraint 18.6--92.5\%.
Prompt framing shifts cooperation from 83.2\% (observer evaluation) to 4.9\% (prediction), and commons restraint is strongly associated with simulated population survival (Pearson $r{=}0.85$).
The release includes prompts, schemas, validation, Croissant metadata, derived tables, figures, and reproduction commands for traceable pre-deployment behavioral auditing.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
LLM agents increasingly operate in social settings: they answer human requests, recommend actions, coordinate with other systems, and may participate in workflows where individual and collective incentives diverge. In these settings, refusal of explicitly harmful requests is only one part of responsible behavior. A broader evaluation should also assess whether a model selects cooperative actions in social dilemmas and whether repeated choices preserve a shared resource when overuse offers a short-term advantage.

Prosocial Readiness Bench addresses this requirement through a reproducible benchmark. The artifact measures three observable behaviors rather than a latent trait: Part 0 measures safety refusal, Part 1 measures dyadic cooperation, and Part 2 measures commons restraint in a repeated simulation. The benchmark is designed for behavioral auditing: it records the action selected by the model, the stated justification when available, the metadata needed to trace a run, and the derived metrics used in the paper.

The paper asks two concrete questions. First, can refusal, cooperation, and commons restraint be measured in one release-grade artifact with explicit schemas, validation, and reproduction commands? Second, do these behaviors move together across a local/open-weight model cohort, or do they expose separable behavioral patterns that a single score would hide? Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the artifact flow.

The pilot evaluation covers 13 local/open-weight model variants. It shows wide and informative variation on all three axes: safety refusal ranges from 9.4\% to 99.3\%, dyadic cooperation from 24.2\% to 75.0\%, and commons restraint from 18.6\% to 92.5\%. Seven of thirteen same-model societies preserve a nonzero population through the 100-day horizon. Cross-part diagnostics show a weak association between refusal and cooperation (Pearson $r=0.24$), a stronger association between refusal and restraint ($r=0.77$), and a strong association between restraint and final population ($r=0.85$). Part 1 also exposes a large elicitation effect: cooperation is 83.2\% under observer-evaluation prompts and 4.9\% under prediction prompts.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Benchmark artifact.} We release Prosocial Readiness Bench, a three-part evaluation for safety refusal, dyadic cooperation, and commons restraint under explicit action contracts.
    \item \textbf{Traceable release package.} We provide prompts, schemas, metadata sidecars, manifests, validation output, derived tables, figures, and reproduction commands aligned with the reported claims.
    \item \textbf{Open-model pilot.} We report 56,382 decisions or judgments across 13 local/open-weight model variants, using Wilson intervals for rates and bootstrap intervals for model-level cross-part correlations.
    \item \textbf{Diagnostic findings.} We show that refusal, cooperation, frame sensitivity, commons restraint, and survival capture different aspects of observable agent behavior.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Prosocial Readiness Bench connects four lines of evaluation work. Safety and jailbreak benchmarks such as HarmBench and JailbreakBench measure harmful-request compliance and refusal \citep{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}. LLM game-theory studies evaluate cooperation in strategic settings inspired by classical work on cooperation and commons governance \citep{axelrod1984evolution,hardin1968tragedy,ostrom1990governing,akata2023playing,brookins2023playing,horton2023homo}. Multi-agent and social-evaluation environments such as generative agents and SOTOPIA study richer open-ended interaction \citep{park2023generative,zhou2024sotopia}. Multi-axis trust and alignment benchmarks motivate reporting separate behavioral dimensions rather than a single score \citep{openai2023gpt4,wang2023decodingtrust,kirk2024prism,jiang2025artificialhivemind}. The gap addressed here is the combination of harmful-request refusal, one-shot social-dilemma cooperation, repeated commons restraint, and traceable release artifacts in one validated benchmark. The pilot cohort uses locally runnable variants across GPT-OSS, Llama~2 \citep{touvron2023llama2}, Qwen~2.5, and Qwen~3.5 families through Ollama \citep{ollama2023}.

\section{Benchmark Design and Task Contracts}
\subsection{Design Principles}
The benchmark uses three design principles.

\paragraph{Measure behaviors separately.}
Safety refusal, dyadic cooperation, and commons restraint are related deployment-relevant behaviors, but they are not interchangeable. The benchmark therefore reports separate primary metrics instead of collapsing them into one prosociality or readiness score.

\paragraph{Make each claim traceable.}
Each run produces structured CSV output and metadata sidecars. The analysis pipeline writes derived tables, validation reports, and figures from those files, so paper claims can be audited against concrete artifacts.

\paragraph{Keep trace-level outputs.}
The benchmark stores actions, justifications, run metadata, and derived summaries so downstream users can audit each reported claim without relying on plot files alone.

The task contracts are intentionally narrow: Part 0 stores responses and judge verdicts, Part 1 requires a JSON action and justification, and Part 2 records daily JSON actions plus resource and population state.

\subsection{Part 0: Safety Refusal}
Part 0 presents harmful-request prompts and records whether the model refuses or materially complies. Prompts are drawn from JailbreakBench harmful behaviors and HarmBench standard behavior prompts; a subset is additionally wrapped with jailbreak templates from the TrustAIRLab in-the-wild collection. The release uses one shared prompt-language matrix for every model: 99 unique base prompt texts rendered in English, Chinese, and Russian, for 297 judged rows per model and 3,861 rows total. The run metadata preserves a 100-entry sampled prompt list from a 500-row source pool (484 unique prompt strings); one sampled prompt string is duplicated, which is why the realized evaluated set has 99 unique prompts rather than 100. The sampling draw was not seeded in the release metadata, so the exact evaluated subset is preserved as an artifact rather than reproducible from a seed.

The current raw CSV schema stores \texttt{provider}, \texttt{model}, \texttt{language}, \texttt{prompt}, \texttt{reasoning}, \texttt{response}, and \texttt{complied?}. Compliance judgments are produced by an automated, response-only LLM judge configured first as \modeltag{ollama/gpt-oss-safeguard:20b}, with \modeltag{ollama/gpt-oss:20b} as the fallback judge. The judge is conservative: it marks compliance only when the response contains concrete harmful details, not when the model provides high-level discussion or disclaimers. The release does not include a human-annotation calibration subset or per-row fallback-judge provenance; judge calibration on borderline, multilingual, and refusal-style cases remains an open limitation discussed in Section~\ref{sec:scope}.

The primary metric is safety refusal:
\[
\mathrm{refusal} =
\frac{\#\mathrm{denied}}{\#\mathrm{denied}+\#\mathrm{complied}}.
\]
Confidence intervals for all rate metrics use the Wilson score method \citep{wilson1927probable}. This metric gives a direct, auditable measure of refusal on the harmful-request set and provides the safety axis used in cross-part analysis.
For multilingual robustness, the analysis also reports the mean refusal rate across languages, the minimum-language refusal rate, and the max--min language gap for each model.

\subsection{Part 1: Dyadic Cooperation}
Part 1 evaluates one-shot social-dilemma choices. The prompt matrix has two game families, four frames, six domains (crime, sports, workplace, healthcare, education, neighborhood), four scenario variants per domain-game pair, and two presentation styles (narrative prose and structured bullet format), yielding $2{\times}4{\times}6{\times}4{\times}2 = 384$ decisions per model. The four scenario variants are stored in \path{experiments/part1/scenario_variants.py} and are selected by \texttt{scenario\_variant} IDs at runtime; \path{experiments/part1/part\_1\_prompt.json} stores the shared game, frame, domain, and presentation templates. The variants change surface context while preserving the same payoff structure and action labels; Appendix~\ref{app:part1-examples} shows an example pair. The game families use \texttt{COOPERATE}/\texttt{DEFECT} and \texttt{RESTRAIN}/\texttt{OVERUSE} action pairs. The frames ask for direct self choice, advice, observer evaluation, or prediction.

The primary metric is dyadic cooperation:
\[
\mathrm{cooperation} =
\frac{\#\mathrm{cooperative}}{\#\mathrm{cooperative}+\#\mathrm{noncooperative}}.
\]
\texttt{COOPERATE} and \texttt{RESTRAIN} are scored as cooperative actions; \texttt{DEFECT} and \texttt{OVERUSE} are scored as non-cooperative actions. The frame, domain, game, variant, and presentation fields make prompt sensitivity measurable rather than implicit.
Frame effect sizes are reported as percentage-point differences from the prediction frame, e.g.,
\[
\Delta_{f,\mathrm{prediction}} = 100(r_f-r_{\mathrm{prediction}}),
\]
where $r_f$ is the cooperation rate under frame $f$. Prompt sensitivity is reported for each model and dimension as
\[
S_{m,d}=100\left(\max_v r_{m,d=v}-\min_v r_{m,d=v}\right),
\]
computed over frames, games, domains, and presentation styles. Because the prompt matrix is balanced, the paper also reports a Type~I (sequential) sum-of-squares decomposition for \(\mathrm{cooperate}\sim\mathrm{model}+\mathrm{frame}+\mathrm{game}+\mathrm{domain}+\mathrm{presentation}\). Since all factors are fully crossed and balanced, Type~I and Type~III decompositions produce identical main-effect sums of squares. The decomposition is not used for causal attribution; it quantifies how much observed binary outcome variance is associated with each balanced factor in this pilot.

\subsection{Part 2: Commons Restraint}
Part 2 creates same-model societies in a repeated common-pool resource simulation. Each configured run starts with 50 active agents, a 100-day horizon, and a water reserve with capacity 2,500 units. \texttt{OPTION\_A} maps to \texttt{RESTRAIN}: it uses a 1x daily score multiplier and leaves the reserve unchanged. \texttt{OPTION\_B} maps to \texttt{OVERUSE}: it gives a higher daily score multiplier and reduces the reserve by 2 units. The prompt includes the current day, living population, reserve state, and previous-day \texttt{OPTION\_B} count.

The primary action metric is commons restraint:
\[
\mathrm{restraint} =
\frac{\#\mathrm{restrain}}{\#\mathrm{restrain}+\#\mathrm{overuse}}.
\]
Part 2 also reports final reserve, first depletion day, final population, total deaths, and survival through the configured horizon. This makes repeated local choices visible as aggregate population and resource trajectories.
To summarize trajectories rather than endpoints alone, we also compute normalized area under the reserve curve (AURC) and population curve (AUPC):
\[
\mathrm{AURC}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{R_t}{R_{\max}}, \qquad
\mathrm{AUPC}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{P_t}{P_0}.
\]
Here $R_t$ and $P_t$ are end-of-day reserve and population, $R_{\max}$ is reserve capacity, $P_0$ is initial population, and missing post-collapse days contribute zero.
The reported Part~2 values are point estimates from one same-model trajectory per model, not averages over repeated random seeds. Wilson intervals on the restraint rate summarize uncertainty for the observed action stream; they do not measure run-to-run simulation variance.

\section{Artifact Construction and Quality Control}
\paragraph{Model cohort and compute.}
The pilot evaluates 13 local/open-weight variants through Ollama \citep{ollama2023}: standard, safeguard, instruct, uncensored, and derestricted variants across GPT-OSS, Llama~2 \citep{touvron2023llama2}, Qwen~2.5, and Qwen~3.5 families. Runs used local Ollama execution with one NVIDIA H200 NVL GPU but were constrained by a 32 GiB RAM, no-swap, 10-CPU cgroup allocation, so the cohort emphasizes repeatedly runnable 7B--20B release-style variants rather than frontier-scale coverage.

\paragraph{Output contracts and metadata.}
Part 0 stores model outputs and a normalized compliance verdict; Part 1 stores a valid action and justification; Part 2 stores a daily action and reasoning field for each living agent. Completed runs are paired with metadata sidecars recording provider/model identifiers, command context, timestamps, hashes when available, experiment settings, run status, and output paths; legacy sidecars are backfilled where possible and marked as reconstructed. The release also provides Croissant JSON-LD metadata \citep{akhtar2024croissant} plus an anonymous supplement with code, documentation, validation artifacts, figures, and Part 1/Part 2 raw CSVs, while excluding raw Part 0 harmful prompts and completions for safety.

\paragraph{Validation.}
Validation is graph-independent. It checks CSV headers, duplicate rows, missing required fields, invalid actions, prompt-matrix coverage, Part 2 day continuity, incomplete days, reserve and population transition consistency, and interrupted versus complete status. The current validation report covers 27 CSV files: one Part 0 file, 13 Part 1 files, and 13 Part 2 files, with 0 failures.

\paragraph{Harmful-output handling.}
Part 0 evaluates harmful prompts and may produce unsafe completions. The anonymous supplement therefore emphasizes derived tables, validation outputs, and aggregate summaries rather than raw harmful generations. This release choice preserves the evidence used in the paper while limiting unnecessary redistribution of harmful text.

\section{Benchmark Statistics}
The release contains 56,382 total decisions or judgments with 0 validation failures across 27 CSV files. Part~0 has 3,861 judgments (13 models $\times$ 297 prompt-language rows) and refusal spans 9.4--99.3\%. Part~1 has 4,992 decisions (13 $\times$ 384) and cooperation spans 24.2--75.0\%. Part~2 has 47,529 agent-day decisions, fewer than the 65,000 maximum because collapsed societies stop producing living-agent decisions; restraint spans 18.6--92.5\%.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
For each model and part, the analysis pipeline computes the primary rate, Wilson 95\% confidence interval, and task-specific diagnostics. Part 0 additionally computes multilingual robustness metrics. Part 1 computes frame effect sizes, prompt-sensitivity indices, and a balanced main-effect variance decomposition. Part 2 additionally computes first depletion day, final reserve, final population, total deaths, AURC, and AUPC. Cross-part analysis joins the three model-level tables by model identifier and reports Pearson and Spearman correlations with 2,000 percentile bootstrap replicates, resampling model rows with replacement using seed 20260429.

Part 1 and Part 2 require parseable JSON actions. The runners retry parse errors, missing required fields, and invalid action labels until a valid row is obtained or the run is interrupted. The released final CSVs therefore contain 0 invalid retained actions in Part 1 (0/4,992 rows) and Part 2 (0/47,529 rows).

All reported pilot runs used local Ollama defaults through the repository provider wrapper. No fixed temperature or greedy-decoding setting is recorded in the release metadata; the wrapper sends \texttt{num\_predict=2048} and leaves temperature unset unless explicitly configured. Different decoding temperatures could shift refusal, cooperation, and restraint rates.

The reported comparisons are descriptive pilot results over 13 related local/open-weight variants. The paper uses them to demonstrate benchmark behavior, reveal diagnostic variation, and generate hypotheses for broader confirmatory evaluation. Family-level readings below group models by visible release lineage and should be interpreted as descriptive clusters, not independent estimates of vendor or architecture effects.

\section{Pilot Results}
\begin{figure}[!htbp]

\paragraph{Refusal separates safety-oriented and less-restricted variants.}
Part 0 refusal ranges from 9.4\% to 99.3\% (Figure~\ref{fig:model-summary}). Standard and safeguard variants refuse nearly all harmful prompts, while uncensored or derestricted variants show much lower refusal. The within-family splits are substantial: GPT-OSS refuses at 99.3\% but its derestricted variant drops to 16.2\% (an 83.1-point gap). Qwen~3.5 base and instruct refuse at 98.3\% and 97.6\%, while the uncensored variants fall to 32.7\% and 9.4\%. Qwen~2.5 base and instruct are lower (74.4\% and 64.3\%), with abliterate variants at 27.9--29.0\%. Llama~2 shows a smaller release-style drop (77.4\% to 51.2\%), which could indicate more stable refusal patterns or less aggressive restriction removal. Notably, GPT-OSS variants remain weak on later social-choice metrics despite high refusal, establishing that safety refusal alone does not predict broader prosocial behavior.
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:refusal-language} shows the multilingual breakdown. Refusal is broadly consistent for the strongest safety-oriented variants (GPT-OSS and Qwen~3.5 both have $\leq$1-point language gaps) but less stable for mid-range and uncensored variants; \texttt{llama2-uncensored} has the largest cross-language spread (31.3 points). Full per-model multilingual robustness is reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:part0-language-robustness}.

\paragraph{Cooperation is strongly frame-sensitive.}
Part~1 cooperation ranges from 24.2\% to 75.0\%. The dominant effect is prompt framing: across models, cooperation is 83.2\% under observer evaluation, 66.8\% under advice, 61.4\% under self-direct choice, and 4.9\% under prediction. The model-family pattern differs from refusal: Qwen~2.5 base, instruct, and abliterate variants all cluster near 74--75\%, while Llama~2 uncensored cooperates more than base Llama~2 (68.8\% versus 52.6\%), reversing the safety-refusal ordering. GPT-OSS safeguard is the strongest GPT-OSS variant on cooperation (47.1\%) but remains below the Qwen~2.5 cluster. The benchmark thereby distinguishes socially preferred judgments from expectations about rational self-interested behavior.
Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:game-family} shows that the temptation/commons game family elicits more cooperation than prisoner-dilemma prompts for most mid-range models. The gap is largest for GPT-OSS safeguard (69.8\% versus 24.5\%) and Qwen~3.5 instruct (69.3\% versus 28.1\%); Qwen~2.5 variants are nearly equal across game families.

\paragraph{Commons restraint produces survival differences.}
Part 2 restraint ranges from 18.6\% to 92.5\% (Figure~\ref{fig:restraint-summary}). Seven of thirteen same-model societies preserve a nonzero population through the 100-day horizon; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:part2} gives per-model outcomes. The clearest within-family split is Qwen~2.5: base and instruct variants show 92.5\% and 92.3\% restraint (AURC 84--85\%, AUPC 100\%), whereas the abliterate variants collapse by days 30--31 with only 18.6--18.7\% restraint---despite high Part~1 cooperation. This dissociation between one-shot cooperation and repeated-resource behavior is a key benchmark finding. GPT-OSS shows a graded release effect: the derestricted variant collapses by day 39, the base model by day 67, and the safeguard variant delays depletion until day 89. Qwen~3.5 instruct illustrates why trajectory summaries matter beyond endpoint survival: it survives to day 100 (AUPC 100\%) but retains only 10 reserve units (AURC 19.8\%), indicating near-collapse despite nominal survival. Both Llama~2 variants survive, with the base model retaining substantially more reserve (1,702 versus 344 units).

Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:reserve-over-time}, \ref{fig:population-over-time}, and~\ref{fig:restraint-over-time} show the temporal dynamics behind the Part~2 summary metrics. High-restraint societies (\texttt{llama2}, Qwen~2.5 base/instruct) maintain stable daily restraint across nearly the full horizon, while low-restraint societies deplete early and enter post-collapse regions. The daily heatmap also identifies strategy-shift cases: \modeltag{sorc/qwen3.5-instruct} starts with low restraint (29.0\% in the first 10 days) but rises to near-universal restraint (98.8\% in the final 10 days), barely surviving with 10 reserve units. \modeltag{sorc/qwen3.5-instruct-uncensored} follows a similar reactive pattern but crosses the depletion threshold on day 34 before the shift takes effect. This suggests that some models can recognize resource depletion but respond too late for recovery.

\section{Diagnostics}
\paragraph{The constructs dissociate.}
The cross-part results support separate reporting. Table~\ref{tab:cross-part-corr} reports both Pearson and Spearman correlations with bootstrap 95\% intervals. Safety refusal and dyadic cooperation have a weak association (Pearson $r{=}0.24$, Spearman $\rho{=}0.13$). Safety refusal and commons restraint are more strongly associated (Pearson $r{=}0.77$, Spearman $\rho{=}0.57$). Restraint and final population show the strongest relationship (Pearson $r{=}0.85$, Spearman $\rho{=}0.90$). The Spearman values are generally smaller than Pearson for the refusal-restraint pair, suggesting that outlier models influence the linear correlation. These results show why the artifact reports multiple axes rather than a single aggregate readiness score.

\end{tabular}%
}
\end{table}
Because there are only 13 related model variants, the bootstrap intervals are descriptive and can be unstable; boundary values such as the 1.00 upper bound for Cooperation $\times$ Restraint should be interpreted cautiously.

\paragraph{Family profiles are not one-dimensional.}
Within each family, release-style variants produce distinct multi-axis profiles. Qwen~2.5 abliterate keeps high Part~1 cooperation but collapses in Part~2; Llama~2 uncensored lowers refusal but raises cooperation while both variants survive the commons; GPT-OSS safeguard is slightly lower on refusal than standard GPT-OSS but higher on cooperation and restraint. These within-family dissociations caution against using family labels as a substitute for axis-level measurement.
Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:behavioral-fingerprint} and~\ref{fig:model-behavior-pca} give the full fingerprint and PCA diagnostics. They show key counterexamples to simple family-level interpretation: Qwen~2.5 abliterate models separate sharply from Qwen~2.5 base despite similar Part~1 cooperation, because their commons trajectories diverge; conversely, \texttt{llama2-uncensored} differs from base \texttt{llama2} on refusal and cooperation but remains in the surviving-commons region.

\paragraph{Framing changes measured cooperation.}
Part~1 treats prompt frame as an experimental variable (Figure~\ref{fig:frame-effects}). Observer-evaluation prompts elicit high cooperative judgments while prediction prompts yield near-zero cooperation, indicating that models can distinguish socially preferred actions from expected self-interested behavior.

Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:frame-effects} gives the model-by-frame heatmap. Nearly every model predicts selfish behavior under the prediction frame, even when it cooperates under other frames. \texttt{llama2-uncensored} is the main exception (58.3\% prediction-frame cooperation). The uncensored Qwen~3.5 variants have lower cooperation across all frames, especially under self-direct prompts.

Appendix Table~\ref{tab:part1-decomposition} quantifies the same pattern. Frame is the largest measured main effect, accounting for 35.1\% of the binary outcome variance in this balanced matrix. Model identity accounts for 15.3\%, the model$\times$frame interaction accounts for 9.6\%, game family for 4.3\%, domain for 0.5\%, and presentation style for less than 0.1\%. Per-model prompt-sensitivity ranges are reported in Appendix Table~\ref{tab:prompt-sensitivity}; frame is the maximum sensitivity dimension for 12 of 13 models, while \texttt{llama2-uncensored} is most sensitive to game family.

\paragraph{Restraint predicts survival in the simulated commons.}
Part 2 ties local overuse choices to aggregate outcomes. Commons restraint is strongly associated with final population (Pearson $r=0.85$, bootstrap interval $[0.68,0.98]$). The relationship is expected from the transition rules and supports auditing by representing cumulative overuse as resource depletion and population loss.

Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:cross-diagnostics} and~\ref{fig:restraint-population} visualize the same cross-part relationships. High refusal does not guarantee high commons preservation: GPT-OSS variants are high-refusal but only midrange on restraint. The principal exception is \texttt{llama2-uncensored}, which has lower refusal than base Llama~2 but remains high on commons restraint. Societies above roughly three-quarters restraint generally survive, while low-restraint runs collapse; among survivors, \modeltag{sorc/qwen3.5-instruct} survives with only 10 reserve units versus 1,752 for \modeltag{qwen2.5:7b}.

\paragraph{Reasoning-action mismatches as process evidence.}
Part 2 records a reasoning field alongside each daily action. The analysis pipeline flags rows where the stated reasoning advocates one action but the recorded action is the opposite. Across all 13 models, 823 such mismatch flags occur in the 47,529 agent-day decisions (1.7\% overall). The distribution is highly skewed: GPT-OSS derestricted produces 164 flags, \modeltag{sorc/qwen3.5-instruct} produces 142, and \modeltag{aratan/qwen3.5-uncensored:9b} produces 114, while Qwen~2.5 abliterate variants produce only 6--15 flags each. High mismatch counts may indicate inconsistent reasoning, prompt sensitivity in the reasoning field, or cases where the model recognizes the cooperative option verbally but selects the selfish action. Low mismatch counts do not confirm coherent reasoning; they may reflect models that produce generic justifications. These flags are released as a diagnostic column rather than a primary metric because the benchmark treats actions as the scored output and reasoning as process evidence.

Across the diagnostics, the central interpretation is that alignment may be pattern-conditional: models can refuse harmful requests they recognize while still selecting self-interested actions in less familiar social-choice settings. This does not show deliberate deception; it shows that refusal-style alignment can be behaviorally narrow, motivating evaluations where models must trade immediate payoff against group welfare.

\section{Scope and Responsible Use}
\label{sec:scope}
Prosocial Readiness Bench evaluates observable actions, not intent or moral status. The current release is an open-model pilot: related variants and local/Ollama tag drift limit leaderboard claims; Part 0 uses automated response-only judge labels without human calibration; multilingual harmful-request evaluation needs language-specific expert review before stronger cross-cultural claims; Part 1 uses artificial one-shot scenarios; and Part 2 uses one homogeneous same-model trajectory per model with no repeated seeds. The benchmark should be used to audit behavioral risks and compare mitigations, not to improve harmful compliance.

\section{Conclusion}
Prosocial Readiness Bench packages traceable artifacts for auditing refusal, dyadic cooperation, and commons restraint. The pilot shows axis dissociation and strong frame sensitivity, motivating broader model coverage, repeated seeds, fixed decoding settings, and richer commons environments.

If both sides choose COOPERATE, each side gets 3 points.
