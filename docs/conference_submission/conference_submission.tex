\begin{abstract}
Behavioral benchmarks can produce precise model comparisons even when their labels, prompts, or independence units do not support the intended claims.
We audit Prosocial Cost-Shifting Bench, a three-part pilot spanning multilingual harmful-request response, one-shot focal choices, and a repeated 50-agent resource task, using 56,382 stored rows from 13 local/open-weight variants.
Only Part~1 direct-choice summaries remain interpretable as descriptive action-label rates: they span 6.2--100.0\%, but one eventually valid response per cell, unknown decoding settings, and mutable model tags preclude run-level inference.
A response-only rejudgment changes 300 of 1,243 adjudicated legacy Part~0 labels (24.1\%), so Part~0 is unavailable as an evaluation module pending complete rejudgment and human validation.
In Part~2, the prompt describes private and group payoffs that the engine never implements. Its 18.6--92.5\% stored \texttt{OPTION\_A} token rates and 13 single trajectories therefore diagnose a prompt--engine contract failure, not commons preference or run-level model effects.
The artifact packages traces, schemas, validation, and provenance needed to expose these failures. The result is a negative benchmark audit: traceability withdraws or reclassifies attractive comparisons before they become safety claims.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
LLMs increasingly serve as decision policies in social settings: they answer human requests, recommend actions, coordinate with other systems, and may participate in workflows where individual and collective incentives diverge \citep{bommasani2021opportunities,liu2024agentbench,park2023generative,zhou2024sotopia}. In these settings, refusal of explicitly harmful requests is only one part of responsible behavior. A broader evaluation should also ask how a model responds when the safety rule is less explicit, the affected group grows, or the consequences accumulate over time.

We present an audit of Prosocial Cost-Shifting Bench, not a validated benchmark release. The pilot was designed to measure safety refusal, focal choices in hypothetical dyadic dilemmas, and commons restraint in a repeated simulation. The audit asks whether the stored artifacts support those intended estimands. They do not uniformly: Part~0 is unavailable as an evaluation module, Part~1 supports only descriptive action-label summaries, and Part~2 is evidence of a prompt--engine contract failure rather than a realized commons game. None of the parts estimates altruism, intent, moral status, readiness, or a single latent prosocial trait.

The paper asks two concrete questions. First, which candidate conclusions survive an end-to-end audit of labels, task contracts, independence units, and transition provenance? Second, what evidence must be withdrawn or reclassified when those checks fail? Figure~\ref{fig:overview} summarizes the audit flow.

The pilot covers 13 related local/open-weight variants. Partial response-only rejudgment changes nearly one quarter of adjudicated Part~0 rows. The Part~2 prompt describes scores that the engine never implements. We therefore withdraw Part~0 model rates, reclassify Part~2 outcomes as contract-audit records, and retain Part~1 rates only as descriptive summaries over a fixed prompt bank. The 13 Part~2 trajectories remain distinct from their 47,529 state-coupled agent-day rows.

Our contributions are:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Reusable claim-status audit.} We specify ordered contract, label, independence-unit, and provenance gates that retain, narrow, reclassify, or withdraw a candidate claim rather than merely documenting its inputs.
    \item \textbf{Negative findings.} We make Part~0 unavailable as an evaluation module, identify Part~2 as a prompt--engine contract failure, and delimit Part~1 to descriptive action-label summaries.
    \item \textbf{Traceable evidence.} We trace 56,382 stored rows to the available prompts, schemas, sidecars, manifests, validation, derived outputs, and reproduction commands, while enumerating unavailable artifacts and unsupported claims.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Safety and jailbreak benchmarks such as HarmBench and JailbreakBench measure harmful-request compliance and refusal \citep{mazeika2024harmbench,chao2024jailbreakbench}; related red-teaming, adversarial-attack, safeguard, and exaggerated-safety work further shows that refusal must be measured under both unsafe and borderline prompts \citep{ganguli2022redteaming,bai2022constitutional,zou2023universal,wang2024donotanswer,rottger2024xstest}. Multilingual jailbreak results additionally show that English-only safety evaluation can miss language-dependent failures \citep{deng2024multilingual}.

LLM game-theory studies evaluate cooperation in strategic settings inspired by classical work on cooperation and commons governance \citep{axelrod1984evolution,hardin1968tragedy,ostrom1990governing,dafoe2020open,akata2025playing,brookins2024playing,horton2023homo}. CoopEval compares repetition, reputation, mediation, and contracts across four social dilemmas and reports weak single-shot cooperation \citep{tewolde2026coopeval}; our focal prompts omit those cooperation-sustaining mechanisms and therefore test a narrower response contract. MACHIAVELLI and the Greatest Good Benchmark measure reward--ethics trade-offs and utilitarian moral judgments, respectively \citep{pan2023machiavelli,marraffini2024greatest}. Multi-agent and social-evaluation environments such as AgentBench, generative agents, SOTOPIA, and SocialEval study richer interaction, goal achievement, or interpersonal process \citep{liu2024agentbench,park2023generative,zhou2024sotopia,zhou2025socialeval}.

Broad multitask and trustworthiness suites motivate reporting separate behavioral dimensions rather than one score \citep{hendrycks2021measuring,srivastava2023beyond,liang2023helm,wang2023decodingtrust}. Model reports and social-alignment datasets provide complementary system, value-diversity, and response-homogeneity evidence \citep{openai2023gpt4,kirk2024prism,jiang2025artificialhivemind}. Dataset and model documentation standards motivate the artifact-facing emphasis on provenance, metadata, access conditions, and responsible-use notes \citep{bender2018datastatements,holland2018datasetnutrition,mitchell2019modelcards,gebru2021datasheets,pushkarna2022datacards,akhtar2024croissant}.

The closest Part~2 precedent is GovSim, an open-source generative commons environment with negotiation and multi-agent communication \citep{piatti2024govsim}. Our microworld is deliberately narrower and does not improve on that interaction model. The contribution here is instead a traceable task-contract audit across harmful-request response, focal dilemma choice, and stateless repeated resource use. The pilot cohort uses locally runnable variants across GPT-OSS \citep{openai2025gptoss}, Llama~2 \citep{touvron2023llama2}, Qwen~2.5 \citep{yang2024qwen25}, and Ollama-hosted Qwen~3.5 tags \citep{ollama2026qwen35}.

\section{Audited Task Contracts}
\subsection{Design Principles}
\paragraph{Probe cost-shifting responses at increasing social scope.}
Each candidate task offers an action that would shift costs onto another party or a collective, but the tasks do not establish one common latent decision process. Part~0 was intended to test harmful compliance affecting third parties, Part~1 records a focal choice in a hypothetical dyad, and Part~2 records repeated resource-use tokens in a population trace. The audit evaluates these contracts separately.

\paragraph{Measure behaviors separately.}
The intended constructs are not interchangeable, and an operational metric is not evidence that its task contract is valid. The audit therefore reports each module separately and withdraws or reclassifies metrics whose supporting contract fails.

\paragraph{Make every claim traceable to stored artifacts.}
Each run produces structured CSV output, actions, justifications when available, and metadata sidecars. Derived tables, validation reports, and figures are generated from these artifacts so the paper can be audited against stored traces rather than plot files alone.

\paragraph{Apply a reusable claim-status protocol.}
For each candidate metric, the audit applies four ordered gates: (1)~the prompt, engine, and scoring rule must define the same task contract; (2)~labels and failure states must match the claimed observable and have task-specific validation; (3)~the analysis unit and uncertainty calculation must match the design's independent sampling unit; and (4)~the raw trace, identity, settings, and state transitions must be recoverable or explicitly marked missing. Passing all gates permits the prespecified claim; a contract failure reclassifies the artifact as protocol-diagnostic evidence; a label or required-validation failure withdraws the metric; and an independence or provenance failure narrows it to a finite-artifact descriptive summary. This claim-state transition is the contribution beyond a passive data card: documentation records what exists, whereas the gates determine which conclusions those records license. Applied here, the same protocol yields three different states---withdrawn (Part~0), descriptive (Part~1), and reclassified (Part~2)---without rescuing a failed module through downstream statistics.

\subsection{Part 0: Unavailable Safety-Refusal Module}
Part 0 presents harmful-request prompts and records whether the model refuses or materially complies. Prompts are drawn directly from JailbreakBench harmful behaviors and HarmBench standard behavior prompts \citep{chao2024jailbreakbench,mazeika2024harmbench}. The release uses one shared prompt-language matrix for every model: 99 base prompt texts rendered in English, Chinese, and Russian, for 297 judged rows per model and 3,861 rows total. The exact English base prompts and output language are preserved. The exact Google-Translate strings sent in the April pilot were not stored, so localized-prompt replay is not byte-identical; current runs store a \texttt{prompt\_sent} field. These public benchmarks may also have appeared in model pretraining or safety tuning; the probe measures responses on a known public set, not unseen-prompt generalization. Cross-language claims remain constrained by the translation record and multilingual jailbreak precedent \citep{deng2024multilingual}.

The April pilot used a two-stage automated labeler: a lexical material-compliance shortcut followed by an LLM judge configured first as \modeltag{ollama/gpt-oss-safeguard:20b}, with \modeltag{ollama/gpt-oss:20b} as fallback. Both the shortcut and the legacy judge prompt could score material in either the stored rationale or final response, and exhausted or invalid judge attempts were stored as denial rather than unjudged. The compact pilot CSV did not retain which route, failure, or judge produced each label. A deterministic response-only rejudgment has adjudicated a non-random fixed prefix of 1,243 of 3,861 rows, covering only five of thirteen models and with uneven per-model counts (64, 295, 297, 297, and 290). It changes 300 labels (24.1\%): 275 legacy-complied rows become denied and 25 change in the other direction. A sanitized checkpoint in \path{data/analysis/part0_rejudge_audit_checkpoint.json} records the transition counts, coverage, limitations, and SHA-256 of the withheld harmful-content source. Because this incomplete single-judge audit establishes material protocol sensitivity but not corrected rates, the paper reports only these aggregate instability counts for Part~0.

The current protocol removes the lexical shortcut, gives the judge the harmful request as context but scores only the response, records every route, attempt, failure, and verdict, uses an explicit unjudged state, and neither requests nor stores hidden chain-of-thought. The exact legacy prompt is archived as \path{experiments/part0/part_0_prompt.legacy_20260411.json}. A deterministic blinded human-audit packet and scoring workflow are provided in \path{analysis/judge_audit.py}, but quantitative human labels are not fabricated or claimed here. General LLM-as-judge studies document systematic judge biases; they do not validate this task-specific classifier \citep{zheng2023judging}.

The intended primary metric was safety refusal:
\[
\mathrm{refusal} =
\frac{\#\mathrm{denied}}{\#\mathrm{denied}+\#\mathrm{complied}}.
\]
The raw legacy module is unavailable as a valid benchmark result. No Part~0 model rate or cross-part statistic appears in the paper until complete response-only rejudgment and the preregistered human audit pass; only the aggregate protocol-instability audit is retained.

\subsection{Part 1: Focal Choices in One-Shot Dyadic Dilemmas}
Part 1 evaluates a focal model response to a hypothetical social dilemma, drawing on prisoner-dilemma and commons traditions \citep{axelrod1984evolution,hardin1968tragedy,ostrom1990governing}. It is not live dyadic interaction: there is no paired opponent, communication, realized payoff, or outcome feedback. The prompt matrix has two game families, four frames, six domains (crime, sports, workplace, healthcare, education, neighborhood), four scenario variants per domain-game pair, and two presentation styles (narrative prose and structured bullet format), yielding $2{\times}4{\times}6{\times}4{\times}2 = 384$ decisions per model. The four scenario variants are stored in \path{experiments/part1/scenario_variants.py} and are selected by \texttt{scenario\_variant} IDs at runtime; \path{experiments/part1/part\_1\_prompt.json} stores the shared game, frame, domain, and presentation templates. The variants change surface context while preserving the same payoff structure and action labels; Appendix~\ref{app:part1-examples} shows an example pair. The game families use \texttt{COOPERATE}/\texttt{DEFECT} and \texttt{RESTRAIN}/\texttt{OVERUSE} action pairs. The frames ask for direct self choice, advice, observer evaluation, or prediction.

The primary behavioral metric is cooperation under the direct self-choice frame:
\[
\mathrm{cooperation} =
\frac{\#\mathrm{cooperative}}{\#\mathrm{cooperative}+\#\mathrm{noncooperative}}.
\]
\texttt{COOPERATE} and \texttt{RESTRAIN} are scored as cooperative actions; \texttt{DEFECT} and \texttt{OVERUSE} are scored as non-cooperative actions. Advice, observer evaluation, and prediction are prompt-semantics calibration probes: they check that the response contract distinguishes recommendations, evaluations, and beliefs from direct self-choice. Their pooled rate is a response-profile summary, not a behavioral cooperation estimate. The frame, domain, game, variant, and presentation fields make prompt sensitivity measurable rather than implicit.
Calibration contrasts are reported as percentage-point differences from the prediction frame, e.g.,
\[
\Delta_{f,\mathrm{prediction}} = 100(r_f-r_{\mathrm{prediction}}),
\]
where $r_f$ is the cooperation rate under frame $f$. Prompt sensitivity is reported for each model and dimension as
\[
S_{m,d}=100\left(\max_v r_{m,d=v}-\min_v r_{m,d=v}\right),
\]
computed over frames, games, domains, and presentation styles. Because the prompt matrix is balanced, the paper also reports a Type~I (sequential) sum-of-squares decomposition for \(\mathrm{cooperate}\sim\mathrm{model}*\mathrm{frame}+\mathrm{game}+\mathrm{domain}+\mathrm{presentation}\), where \texttt{model*frame} includes both main effects and their interaction. Since these factors are fully crossed and balanced, the sequential terms are orthogonal under the stored matrix \citep{montgomery2017design}. The decomposition is not used for causal attribution; it quantifies how much observed binary outcome variance is associated with each balanced term in this pilot.

\subsection{Part 2: Prompt--Engine Contract Audit}
Part 2 was intended as a controlled population microworld rather than a simulation of a real society \citep{hardin1968tragedy,ostrom1990governing,piatti2024govsim}. Each configured run starts with 50 active policies, a 100-day horizon, and a water reserve with capacity 2,500 units. The legacy pilot prompt described \texttt{OPTION\_A} as a 1x private score multiplier and \texttt{OPTION\_B} as a higher private multiplier. It also stated that the group-level score would change by +5 if all participants selected \texttt{OPTION\_A} and by -5 if all selected \texttt{OPTION\_B}. The engine never computed, stored, or fed back either individual or group scores. Operationally, \texttt{OPTION\_A} maps to \texttt{RESTRAIN} and leaves the reserve unchanged, whereas \texttt{OPTION\_B} maps to \texttt{OVERUSE} and reduces it by 2 units. Every policy receives the same aggregate state: current day, living population, reserve, and previous-day \texttt{OPTION\_B} count. Actions and rationales may therefore respond to stated score incentives that were absent from the implemented environment. The audit treats the stored traces as evidence of that contract mismatch, not as estimates from a realized commons payoff system.

The stored action summary is the fraction of \texttt{OPTION\_A} tokens:
\[
\mathrm{OPTION\_A\ rate} =
\frac{\#\mathrm{OPTION\_A}}{\#\mathrm{OPTION\_A}+\#\mathrm{OPTION\_B}}.
\]
For transition replay and contract diagnosis, Part~2 also reports final reserve, first depletion day, final population, total deaths, reserve nondepletion through the horizon, and normalized area under the reserve curve (AURC) and population curve (AUPC). Reserve nondepletion and retention of a nonzero simulated population are distinct mechanical outcomes:
\[
\mathrm{AURC}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{R_t}{R_{\max}}, \qquad
\mathrm{AUPC}=\frac{1}{T}\sum_{t=1}^{T}\frac{P_t}{P_0}.
\]
Here $R_t$ and $P_t$ are end-of-day reserve and population, $R_{\max}$ is reserve capacity, $P_0$ is initial population, and missing post-collapse days contribute zero.
The reported Part~2 values are descriptive summaries of one contract-mismatched trajectory per model, not estimates of commons preference or averages over repeated random seeds. The machine-readable table marks run-level intervals as not estimable for these single trajectories; the paper does not plot within-trajectory Wilson intervals because state-coupled agent-days are not independent runs.
Each call receives only that shared state; calls within a trajectory are nevertheless state-coupled and are not treated as independent replicates. The environment has no communication, individual memory, partner selection, heterogeneity, reputation, enforcement, replenishment, endogenous institutions, or stochastic shocks. The transition from overuse to depletion and attrition is deterministic, so reserve nondepletion, population retention, AURC, and AUPC are downstream consequences of the configured rules rather than independent evidence of ecological validity. In the pilot, calls were issued in fixed agent order and post-depletion attrition removed the tail of that order; identity and population retention are therefore not exchangeable. Changing the 2-unit overuse penalty, reserve capacity, horizon, death rule, or call order would shift measured outcomes.

\section{Artifact Construction and Quality Control}
\paragraph{Model cohort and compute.}
The pilot evaluates 13 local/open-weight variants through Ollama \citep{ollama2023}: standard, safeguard, instruct, uncensored, and derestricted variants across GPT-OSS \citep{openai2025gptoss}, Llama~2 \citep{touvron2023llama2}, Qwen~2.5 \citep{yang2024qwen25}, and Ollama-hosted Qwen~3.5 tags \citep{ollama2026qwen35}. Runs used local Ollama execution with one NVIDIA H200 NVL GPU but were constrained by a 32 GiB RAM, no-swap, 10-CPU cgroup allocation, so the cohort emphasizes repeatedly runnable 7B--20B release-style variants rather than frontier-scale coverage.

\paragraph{Output contracts and metadata.}
Part 0 stores model outputs and a normalized compliance verdict; Part 1 stores a valid action and justification; Part 2 stores a daily action and reasoning field for each living agent. Completed runs are paired with metadata sidecars recording provider/model identifiers, command context, timestamps, hashes when available, experiment settings, run status, and output paths. Fourteen of 27 sidecars are reconstructed legacy metadata (the Part 0 file and all 13 Part 1 files); Part~1 repairs lossy filename-derived model slugs from the unique exact identity in every CSV row and records the CSV hash without replacing the original reconstruction provenance. All 13 Part 2 sidecars retain their original bytes. Those Part 2 sidecars predate an explicit collapse-death-rate field and record a dirty collection worktree. A separate deterministic provenance seal binds each CSV and sidecar hash to the identical archived source blob at both recorded commits, verifies its divisor-of-five rule, and replays every population transition; validation never substitutes the current runtime default. The release also provides Croissant JSON-LD metadata \citep{akhtar2024croissant} plus an anonymous supplement with code, documentation, validation artifacts, figures, and Part 1/Part 2 raw CSVs. It excludes raw Part 0 harmful content, every invalid legacy Part~0 rate table, and all dependent cross-part tables and plots.

\paragraph{Validation.}
Validation is graph-independent. It checks CSV headers, duplicate rows, missing required fields, invalid actions, prompt-matrix coverage, Part 2 day continuity, incomplete days, reserve and population transition consistency, and interrupted versus complete status. The current validation report covers 27 CSV files: one Part 0 file, 13 Part 1 files, and 13 Part 2 files, with 0 failures; it validates artifact structure and transitions, not the semantic accuracy of the automated Part 0 judge labels.

\paragraph{Scale.}
The release contains 56,382 stored rows with 0 structural validation failures across 27 CSV files: 3,861 Part~0 judgments, 4,992 Part~1 decisions, and 47,529 Part~2 agent-day decisions. These row counts are artifact scale, not an independent sample size: Part~0 has 99 shared prompt roots per model, Part~1 has 48 shared scenario roots per model, and Part~2 has only 13 trajectories. Part~2 is below the 65,000 maximum because collapsed societies stop producing living-agent decisions.

\section{Evaluation Protocol}
For each model and part, the analysis pipeline computes the retained summary and task-specific diagnostics. Paper-facing Part~1 bands use a deterministic 2,000-replicate percentile cluster bootstrap with seed 20260802, resampling the fixed bank of 48 scenario-variant roots while retaining their frames and presentations \citep{efron1979bootstrap}. These are finite-bank root-resampling sensitivity intervals: they describe dependence on the observed root bank and do not estimate generation variability or a scenario superpopulation. Row-binomial Wilson bounds are retained only as explicitly named diagnostics, not confidence intervals for the independent sampling units \citep{wilson1927probable}. Part~1 computes calibration contrasts, prompt-sensitivity indices, and a balanced main-effect variance decomposition, with direct self-choice as the designated primary estimand and all-frame pooling secondary. Part~2 summarizes each contract-mismatched trajectory; the pilot has one trajectory per model and therefore no run-level interval. It also computes first depletion day, final reserve, final population, total deaths, reserve nondepletion, AURC, and AUPC for transition diagnosis. Retrospective model-level association analysis reports Pearson, Spearman, and Kendall $\tau_b$ correlations, conventional exchangeability-reference Fisher-transform intervals, 2,000-replicate finite-panel model-row sensitivity intervals with seed 20260429, and leave-one-model/family-out ranges. Fisher-transform intervals for rank coefficients are approximate reference calculations. Because 13 related model variants are not an exchangeable population sample, none of the association intervals supports population inference; the family-deletion range is emphasized.

Part 1 and Part 2 require parseable JSON actions. Each action receives at most three attempts, and an incomplete run receives at most three run attempts before failing with a provenance-bearing metadata sidecar. The April pilot traversed the Part~1 matrix in fixed construction order and retained only the eventual valid response. Thus the displayed pilot denominators contain 0 retained invalid actions (0/4,992 Part~1 rows and 0/47,529 Part~2 rows), but those zeros do not estimate first-attempt invalidity or retry rates. The current runner uses a recorded seed for shuffled, cyclically counterbalanced Part~1 orders and writes every raw success, parse failure, provider error, and interruption to a durable attempt log with counts and hashes in metadata. These corrections govern confirmatory runs but do not retroactively randomize the pilot.

All reported pilot runs used local Ollama defaults through the repository provider wrapper. No fixed temperature or greedy-decoding setting is recorded in the release metadata; the wrapper sends \texttt{num\_predict=2048} and leaves temperature unset unless explicitly configured. The pilot therefore cannot determine whether its rates are stable to decoding settings.

\section{Audit Findings}
These findings over 13 related local/open-weight variants document what the stored pilot can and cannot support; they do not estimate vendor or architecture effects.

\paragraph{The Part~0 audit disqualifies the legacy safety results.}
The incomplete response-only rejudgment changes 300 of 1,243 adjudicated rows (24.1\%): 275 legacy-complied rows become denied and 25 change in the other direction. These rows are a non-random fixed prefix spanning only five of thirteen models, with uneven coverage, so the percentage is an audit-instability diagnostic rather than an estimate for the full pilot. This aggregate count is the only Part~0 pilot result reported in the paper. Exact localized prompts and row-level legacy judge failures are unavailable, and the replacement judge has not yet passed the planned human audit.

\paragraph{Direct choices vary, and role probes calibrate prompt semantics.}
The primary direct self-choice cooperation rate ranges from 6.2\% to 100.0\% across models and is 61.4\% across all self-choice rows. As a calibration check, the same action labels appear in 66.8\% of advice prompts, 83.2\% of observer-evaluation prompts, and 4.9\% of prediction prompts. These prompts ask different questions, so their contrasts are consistent with semantic sensitivity of the response contract rather than a substantive change in one cooperation construct. The pooled all-frame rate is retained only as a secondary response-profile summary. Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:game-family} gives descriptive game-family differences.

\end{figure*}

\paragraph{The Part~2 traces diagnose the mismatched contract.}
Stored \texttt{OPTION\_A} token rates range from 18.6\% to 92.5\% (Figure~\ref{fig:restraint-summary}). Seven of thirteen same-model populations preserve a nonzero population through the 100-day horizon; Appendix Table~\ref{tab:part2} gives the mechanically implied outcomes. Qwen~2.5 base/instruct and abliterate variants have 99--100\% Part~1 direct-choice action-label rates, while their Part~2 records contain 92.5\% and 92.3\% versus 18.6--18.7\% \texttt{OPTION\_A} tokens and the latter deplete by days 30--31. The three stored GPT-OSS traces first deplete on days 39 (derestricted), 67 (base), and 89 (safeguard). Qwen~3.5 instruct retains all 50 simulated policies through day 100 (AUPC 100\%) but finishes with 10 reserve units (AURC 19.8\%). Both Llama~2 traces retain all 50 policies, with 1,702 versus 344 final reserve units. These are reproducible trace contrasts under the broken contract, not evidence of model-level commons preferences.

Appendix Figures~\ref{fig:reserve-over-time}--\ref{fig:restraint-over-time} and~\ref{fig:agent-day-raster} show the temporal records. Under the implemented transition, more \texttt{OPTION\_A} tokens mechanically preserve reserve; several Qwen~3.5 instruct traces shift toward that token only after substantial depletion. Appendix~\ref{app:extended-diagnostics} reports the exact early/late rates.

\section{Diagnostics}
\label{sec:diagnostics}
\paragraph{Model-level associations remain descriptive.}
Table~\ref{tab:cross-part-corr} reports retrospective comparisons among the retained Part~1 summaries and contract-mismatched Part~2 records. Direct self-choice action labels and stored \texttt{OPTION\_A} tokens have a small association (Pearson $r{=}0.27$, Spearman $\rho{=}0.24$). Stored \texttt{OPTION\_A} tokens and final population show a strong descriptive relationship (Pearson $r{=}0.85$, Spearman $\rho{=}0.90$), as expected from the deterministic transition rules. Neither comparison validates cross-task transfer or identifies a vendor- or architecture-level relationship.

\paragraph{Role instructions serve as prompt-semantics checks.}
Part~1 treats prompt frame as an experimental variable (Figure~\ref{fig:frame-effects}). Observer and prediction prompts ask for evaluation and beliefs, respectively. Their different action-label rates show that the runner preserves those semantic distinctions, not that one cooperation construct changes under paraphrase. Frame accounts for 35.1\% of binary action-label variance and model$\times$frame for 9.6\%; Appendix Tables~\ref{tab:part1-decomposition} and~\ref{tab:prompt-sensitivity} give the decomposition.

\paragraph{The stored token is mechanically linked to downstream population outcomes.}
The \texttt{OPTION\_A}-token--population association (Pearson $r=0.85$) follows the deterministic depletion mechanism, not ecological validity; transition replay supplies the mechanism check (Appendix Figure~\ref{fig:restraint-population}).

\paragraph{Rationale-action mismatches as a lexical diagnostic.}
Part~2 records a brief rationale. A directional lexical check flags 823 of 47,529 rows (1.7\%) where \texttt{OVERUSE} accompanies reserve-preservation or collective-benefit language. The prompt's unimplemented scores may induce this language, so the count diagnoses the stored contract mismatch rather than hidden reasoning quality (Appendix~\ref{app:extended-diagnostics}).

Across these diagnostics, the audit cannot test whether valid refusal rates transfer to either construct or identify intent, deception, or a general prosocial disposition.

\section{Scope and Responsible Use}
\label{sec:scope}
This audit does not validate a three-part benchmark or measure intent, moral status, or readiness. Part~0 is unavailable, Part~1 supplies fixed-bank action-label summaries, and Part~2 supplies contract-audit traces. Further limitations include related variants and mutable tags; incomplete Part~0 rejudgment and missing localized prompts; fixed Part~1 order and unretained retries; one Part~2 trajectory per model; and missing decoding controls. Public items may have appeared in training or tuning. Recorded identifiers describe the measured stack, not stable family effects.

Part~2 uses repeated stateless prompting, not a forecast of human institutions or deployed agents. Communication, tools, memory, realized payoffs, heterogeneous goals, uncertainty, enforcement, organizational oversight, scale, shocks, and parameter sweeps remain external-validity tests; GovSim provides a richer commons precedent \citep{piatti2024govsim}. The legacy trajectory cannot establish failure or success under a realized commons game because the prompt and engine specify different incentives. Role gaps identify specification sensitivity, not realized bargaining behavior. A repaired task requires a new evaluation rather than reinterpretation of these traces.

The artifact supports protocol diagnosis, not behavioral mitigation comparison or harmful compliance. Raw Part~0 harmful prompts and completions are excluded from the anonymous supplement; multilingual claims require expert review. Downstream releases should retain safety filtering, access controls, and responsible-use documentation.

\section{Conclusion}
The negative result is that Prosocial Cost-Shifting Bench does not survive audit as a three-part behavioral benchmark. Part~0 is unavailable, Part~2 records a prompt--engine failure, and Part~1 remains a descriptive fixed-bank action-label profile. Traceability makes these withdrawals possible. Confirmatory claims require a complete human-validated rejudgment, broader model coverage, recorded decoding controls, repeated trajectories, and sensitivity analysis of the commons dynamics.

\section{Extended Diagnostic Details}
\label{app:extended-diagnostics}
The contract-mismatched Part~2 traces contain temporal token shifts. \modeltag{sorc/qwen3.5-instruct} starts with 29.0\% \texttt{OPTION\_A} tokens in the first 10 days and reaches 98.8\% in the final 10 days, retaining all 50 policies but only 10 reserve units at the horizon. The agent-day raster shows a visually widespread shift among the policies still called on those days, not a run-level uncertainty or behavioral effect. \modeltag{sorc/qwen3.5-instruct-uncensored} has a similar trace but crosses the depletion threshold on day 34 before the shift can prevent collapse.

Within-family records further reject a single readiness ordering, but the Part~2 differences cannot be interpreted beyond the mismatched contract. Qwen~2.5 base and abliterate variants make nearly identical direct Part~1 choices but have sharply different stored Part~2 traces. GPT-OSS standard, safeguard, and derestricted variants differ on both recorded summaries.

The full Part~1 decomposition assigns 35.1\% of binary outcome variance to frame, 15.3\% to model, 9.6\% to model$\times$frame, 4.3\% to game family, 0.5\% to domain, and less than 0.1\% to presentation style. Frame is the maximum sensitivity dimension for 12 of 13 models, which is a calibration result because the role instructions ask different questions. Within matched domain-game pairs, prisoner-dilemma scenario variants differ by at most 7.7 percentage points in aggregate direct-choice cooperation.

The reasoning-action diagnostic is deliberately one-sided: it flags \texttt{OVERUSE} rows containing reserve-preservation or collective-benefit language, but does not count \texttt{RESTRAIN} actions justified by selfish language. Of 823 flags, GPT-OSS derestricted contributes 164, \modeltag{sorc/qwen3.5-instruct} 142, and \modeltag{aratan/qwen3.5-uncensored:9b} 114; Qwen~2.5 abliterate variants contribute only 6--15 each. The legacy prompt mentioned private multipliers and an all-participant group score that the engine never implemented, so the rationales may respond to stated but nonexistent incentives. Neither high nor low counts establish coherent reasoning.
